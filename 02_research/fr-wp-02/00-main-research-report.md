## 1. Scope and governance

This work package is foundational business-model and economic research only. It evaluates three deliberately separated lanes:

1. **Lane A — Ekewaka Select / Trade Publishing**: publisher-funded, selective acquisition.
2. **Lane B — Ekewaka Partner / Hybrid / Co-Investment**: research-only author-subsidized publishing model that must not be launched without later Human Owner approval.
3. **Lane C — Ekewaka Author Services**: fee-for-service work for authors who remain their own publisher unless another relationship is explicitly contracted.

## 2. Executive findings

### 2.1 Three distinct economic businesses exist

The evidence strongly supports preserving the three-lane architecture, but only if the lanes remain operationally, contractually, financially, and publicly distinct.

- **Trade publishing** is an investment business. The publisher selects titles, funds production and approved publishing costs, controls publisher editions and distribution accounts, assumes sales/returns risk, and pays the author royalties from sales and licensed rights.
- **Hybrid/co-investment publishing** is an author-subsidized publishing business. A defensible hybrid is not merely a service company using the word “publisher.” IBPA’s current criteria require real vetting, transparent business practices, understandable contracts, publication under the hybrid publisher’s own imprint and ISBNs, professional production, distribution, sales responsibility, rights management, respectable sales, and royalties above standard trade levels.
- **Author services** is a professional-services business. The author pays for defined deliverables; the author should normally retain rights, publishing identity, ISBN/account control, and retail/distribution account control. Provider revenue is primarily fees for labor/vendor work rather than participation in title economics.

These are not simply different package tiers. They create different duties, risk allocation, cash-flow patterns, margin structures, author expectations, and reputational exposure.

### 2.2 Lane A is supportable but must be conservative

A selective small press can use POD and direct-sales channels to reduce inventory risk, but it cannot eliminate title-level investment risk. Current KDP economics show Amazon print sales generally paying the publisher account 50% or 60% of list price depending on list price, less print cost; Expanded Distribution pays 40% of list price less print cost. KDP pays standard print royalties approximately 60 days after month-end and Expanded Distribution later. IngramSpark exposes publisher compensation as a function of list price, wholesale discount and print cost; returnable titles can generate later charges against publisher compensation.

For Ekewaka, the principal Lane A financial problem is therefore not “what royalty should we offer?” but **what contribution margin remains after channel discount, print cost, returns, author royalty, title investment, marketing, overhead and cash timing**.

The Authors Guild model trade contract is useful as an industry benchmark rather than a prescription. It identifies traditional hardcover royalties commonly starting at 10% and escalating, and notes that many small/medium presses use net receipts as the royalty base. It also identifies 25% of publisher net receipts as the common contemporary ebook benchmark. The Guild’s author-copy model provides at least 25 copies of each print edition and often permits additional copies around half cover price or cost plus shipping. Ekewaka should not adopt any of those terms automatically; FR-WP-06 must test their affordability across channels and formats.

### 2.3 Lane B is economically possible but should remain research-only

IBPA explicitly defines hybrid publishing as author-subsidized while still requiring the publisher to perform genuine publishing functions. That creates a high operational bar. A defensible hybrid must have:

- real editorial/market vetting;
- publisher responsibility for production quality;
- publisher-owned imprint/ISBN for the published edition;
- meaningful distribution and sales activity;
- transparent author contribution and contract;
- higher-than-trade author economics;
- no misleading implication that the author was publisher-funded;
- a credible record of sales appropriate to the niche.

Current named comparators demonstrate the range. She Writes Press publicly lists a $12,000 all-inclusive publishing package as of January 2026 and includes trade distribution, design, proofreading, warehousing, fulfillment and ongoing metadata/project management. Greenleaf Book Group describes a selective hybrid model in which authors retain rights and creative control but invest in the process; pricing is customized after acceptance. These examples demonstrate that credible hybrid publishing can be materially more expensive and operationally intensive than low-cost “publishing packages.”

The principal Ekewaka risk is not merely financial loss. It is **trade/services/hybrid confusion**. If Ekewaka charges authors while using publisher-owned ISBNs and publisher distribution accounts, it is entering a publisher relationship with duties materially different from Lane C. For that reason FIN-01 recommends Lane B remain research-only through FR-WP-06, legal/contract review, distribution validation, and an explicit later Human Owner gate.

### 2.4 Lane C can produce earlier revenue but is labor-sensitive

Public author-service benchmarks range from low-cost bundled offers to several-thousand-dollar professional production engagements. Destiny House Publishing currently presents self-publishing packages with public list prices from $799 to $1,900 on its main package page, although individual product pages retrieved during this work displayed lower sale/deposit figures for certain packages; this inconsistency itself is a reminder that competitor web pricing is volatile and must be rechecked before use. Destiny House states that authors maintain publishing rights and set retail price, which is consistent with a services model.

BookBaby states that professional full-service self-publishing commonly lands around $2,000–$7,000 depending on editing, design and printing, and separately describes typical full-service bundles around $1,500–$3,000. Reedsy’s 2026 marketplace data estimates professional editing for an 80,000-word manuscript around $2,160–$5,040 and average cover design around $880 (most projects $625–$1,250).

These figures establish a market context, not Ekewaka pricing. The key finding for Lane C is that **editing hours dominate cost quickly**. Any package containing open-ended developmental/copy editing, unlimited revisions, custom web/video work, or included print quantities can become unprofitable unless word/page limits, revision limits, vendor allowances, scope-change rules and payment milestones are explicit.

### 2.5 Direct-to-consumer economics can materially improve contribution

Direct sales remove or reduce the traditional wholesale/retail share but add payment processing, fulfillment, shipping, customer-service and return/refund obligations. Lulu Direct illustrates the structure clearly: the merchant controls the customer sale and separately pays Lulu for printing, fulfillment, shipping and taxes. Lulu’s August 27, 2026 guidance treats those as two transactions. Lulu’s public example shows a $22 retail price against $12.50 print/shipping/fulfillment cost producing $9.50 before the merchant’s payment-processing, platform, support, marketing, tax and overhead costs.

This supports treating direct sales as a separate FR-WP-06 channel rather than applying a single blended royalty/margin assumption to all sales.

## 3. Business-model distinctions

| Dimension | Lane A — Select / Trade | Lane B — Partner / Hybrid | Lane C — Author Services |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who pays production | Publisher | Author subsidy plus publisher contribution/responsibility | Author/customer |
| Primary financial risk | Publisher | Shared; structure-specific | Provider mainly bears labor/scope/vendor risk; author bears publishing/commercial risk |
| Copyright posture | Author typically retains copyright; licenses defined publishing rights | Must be contract-defined; reputable hybrids commonly preserve author ownership while publisher controls published edition | Author retains rights; provider gets narrow service licenses |
| ISBN for published edition | Publisher ISBN | Reputable hybrid normally publisher imprint/ISBN under IBPA criteria | Normally author-owned ISBN/publishing identity; avoid converting service customer into publisher imprint by default |
| Retail/distribution accounts | Publisher | Publisher/hybrid | Author/customer wherever practicable |
| Revenue source | Book/right sales | Author contribution + book/right sales | Service fees; possibly pass-through vendor charges |
| Returns exposure | Publisher | Publisher/shared contractually | Normally author/customer if using own distribution account |
| Author economics | Contract royalty; possible advance | Higher-than-standard royalty/proceeds expected because author invested | Author keeps title revenue after platform/channel costs |
| Core success test | Sustainable title/catalog economics | Sustainable title economics + compliance with professional hybrid standards | Service gross margin + quality + customer outcomes without sales guarantees |

## 4. Platform/channel economic foundations

### KDP print

Current KDP guidance states:

- paperbacks on Amazon marketplaces receive 50% or 60% of list price depending on price threshold, less printing cost;
- for Amazon.com the 60% tier begins at $9.99 as of retrieval;
- Expanded Distribution uses 40% of list price less printing cost;
- print cost varies by page count, ink/paper and marketplace;
- KDP POD means no print inventory is required up front;
- standard print royalties are generally paid approximately 60 days after month-end; Expanded Distribution is slower.

These figures are platform terms, not publisher gross margin. Author royalty, returns, title investment and overhead still sit below publisher compensation.

### IngramSpark

Current IngramSpark tools calculate publisher compensation using book specification, list price and discount. Ingram’s public materials emphasize that wholesale discount affects publisher compensation. IngramSpark also allows returnability choices; returnable books can result in return charges deducted from publisher compensation, and “Yes — Deliver” can add shipping/handling exposure. FR-WP-03 should revalidate exact discount/returns choices and current price sheets before FR-WP-06 finalization.

### Lulu and direct sales

Lulu’s current distribution model says 50% of print retail price goes to distribution channels, then Lulu takes 20% of the remaining gross profit after print cost, leaving the creator 80% of that gross profit. Lulu Direct is different: the merchant receives the customer payment and separately pays Lulu’s print, shipping/handling, fulfillment and tax charges. This makes direct contribution structurally different from wholesale contribution.

## 5. Royalties and royalty bases — benchmark, not recommendation

Observed benchmark points:

- Authors Guild model trade contract: traditional hardcover benchmark 10% / 12.5% / 15% of list price by sales tiers for major-style trade treatment.
- Authors Guild commentary: small/medium publishers increasingly use net receipts rather than list price; the model shows 10% / 12.5% / 15% of net receipts for hardcover as one benchmark structure.
- Authors Guild: most traditional publishers currently pay 25% of publisher net receipts on ebooks.
- IBPA: a hybrid publisher using royalties should pay higher-than-standard royalties because the author is investing.

For Ekewaka, no royalty can be selected independently of channel contribution. A royalty based on list price can become disproportionately expensive in deeply discounted or high-return channels. A net-receipts base can better track actual publisher revenue but must be narrowly and transparently defined. These are FR-WP-06 and later contract-review questions, not decisions made here.

## 6. Consumer-protection and reputation findings

The Authors Guild’s 2026 hybrid guidance warns against extraordinary-result promises, vague marketing deliverables, non-negotiable one-sided provisions, required author-copy purchases and scams impersonating legitimate publishers. Its April 2026 scam guidance reiterates that traditional publishers do not charge authors up front, while legitimate hybrids require careful diligence.

The Society of Authors/Writers’ Guild of Great Britain investigation reports widespread concerns in paid-for publishing: aggressive marketing, unclear contracts, poor-value services and large author losses in its surveyed population. Its principles emphasize business-model disclosure, itemized costs, predicted return context, production capability and marketing specificity.

These sources are not U.S. legal advice, but they are highly relevant reputation and ethical-design evidence. Ekewaka should treat “no guaranteed sales/bestseller/bookstore placement,” itemized deliverables, cancellation/refund transparency, and clear lane labeling as hard design requirements.

## 7. Preliminary implications for Ekewaka

### Supported assumptions

- A selective publisher-funded Lane A is a recognizable and defensible publishing model.
- A separate fee-for-service Lane C is also recognizable and can preserve author publishing control.
- Hybrid is a real model but has a materially higher credibility and operational standard than merely sharing costs.
- Publisher-owned ISBNs fit Lane A and, if Lane B is ever approved, are consistent with IBPA hybrid criteria.
- Author-owned ISBN/publishing accounts are the cleaner default for Lane C.
- KDP/Ingram/direct channels have materially different contribution margins and cash timing and must be modeled separately.

### Assumptions requiring modification

- “No advance” may be economically sensible for a new press, but cannot be adopted as a blanket final contract policy without acquisition/contract review.
- “Higher author economics” for Lane B needs an explicit measurable standard relative to Lane A and must be tested against the author contribution.
- Service packages cannot be validated from competitor sticker prices alone; internal labor, contractor, revisions, included copies and support burden must drive floors.

### Remain unresolved

- Exact Lane A royalty schedules by format and channel.
- Whether a Lane B model can meet IBPA-level standards at Ekewaka’s initial scale.
- Exact Ingram wholesale discount/returnability policy.
- Printer/vendor costs and quantity breakpoints, owned by FR-WP-03.
- ISBN and rights treatment details, owned by FR-WP-05.
- Final service package scope/pricing, owned by SERV-01 after FIN-01 economic validation.
- Tax/GRT/accounting treatment, requiring CPA review.
- Contract definitions of net receipts, returns reserves, rights, reversion and audit, requiring publishing counsel.

## 8. Recommendation

FIN-01 recommends advancing the evidence package to independent verification with the following preliminary design posture:

1. **Lane A:** proceed as the core publishing model for later architecture, using conservative acquisition economics and channel-specific contribution modeling.
2. **Lane B:** remain **research-only**; do not design for launch until FR-WP-06, legal review, professional-standard mapping, operational capacity and Human Owner approval exist.
3. **Lane C:** proceed to later SERV-01 architecture as a clearly separated professional-services business; use author-controlled ISBN/accounts by default and impose explicit scope/revision/vendor limits.
4. **FR-WP-06:** model channels and lanes separately; do not use one generic “royalty margin.”

These are recommendations only and do not approve prices, royalties, packages, contracts, acquisitions or launch.
